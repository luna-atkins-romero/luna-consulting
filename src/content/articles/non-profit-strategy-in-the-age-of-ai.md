It is a strange time to be a strategy consultant. Not long ago, a client's first draft of "what a strategic plan should look like" came from their own previous Strategy, a peer organisation or a template. However, increasingly, clients now arrive with a very clear picture of what Strategy should look and sound like because of what an AI agent has shown them.

I am not here to complain about that. I have written about the opportunities of AI in a recent article ([AI for good?](https://www.lunaconsulting.ie/articles/ai-for-good)). But it has made me reflect on what Strategy is for, and on my own value as a consultant to the charity sector.

# The Strategy that reads perfectly

Ask AI for a charity strategic plan and you will get something polished in seconds: five pillars, confident verbs, a tidy vision statement, a neat set of fluffy sounding values. It will look like a strategic plan. It will read like a strategic plan.

That is the problem. **A document that looks like Strategy is not the same as a Strategy.** Strategy is a set of choices about what you will do, what you will stop doing, and why. A generic plan avoids choices, and choices are the hard part.

# The process is the point

It is easy to think of a strategic plan as the thing you end up with: a document for the funder, the website and the filing cabinet. But the value of a Strategy lies as much in how it is made as in what it says. The process is where a Board debates what matters most, where staff, volunteers, service users and funders are listened to, where trade-offs are argued through, and where buy-in is built. **The final document is a record of those conversations. AI can produce the record in seconds. It cannot have the conversations.**

# Where it goes wrong

**It isn't rooted in what your stakeholders want.** AI can tell you what charities like yours typically prioritise. It cannot tell you what your service users, volunteers, funders and staff need from you, or what they are frustrated by. That only comes from listening: surveys, focus groups, workshops, and the sometimes uncomfortable conversations. A Strategy that skips this may look complete, but the people it is meant to serve are missing from it.

**It isn't tied to reality.** Most non-profits are working with limited staff, uncertain funding and boards of volunteers. A Strategy written without that context can be full of ambitions that the organisation has no capacity to deliver. I have seen plans, long before AI, that promised the earth. AI makes it easier to produce them faster and with more conviction.

**It undermines accountability.** When a Strategy emerges from real discussion, there are people who own it, argued for it and will be held to it. When it emerges from a prompt, it is easy for nobody to feel it is theirs. Boards and leadership teams still carry the legal and moral responsibility for the direction of the organisation, and that is best exercised through deliberation, not by signing off a finished draft.

**It compromises your authenticity.** The best strategies I have worked on sound like the organisation: its history, its values, and the communities it comes from. AI-generated Strategies can only be a rehashing of someone else's work. A charity that has spent decades earning trust and respect through good work should not sound like every other charity.

# So what does a consultant add?

I have asked myself this through my consulting career. And reassuringly, my answer hasn't changed: a consultant designs and leads the Strategy process. What clients still need is:

- **Facilitation:** helping boards and teams navigate sticky conversations, competing demands and expectations
- **Challenge:** a critical friend asking "is that what your stakeholders said they want?", "is that ambitious enough?", and "can you really do both?"
- **Evidence:** grounding decisions in what your own stakeholders and data say, or drawing on experience across the sector
- **Creativity:** helping you think innovatively and bravely about new solutions to new or old problems
- **Accountability:** building ownership, measures and review opportunities so the Strategy is a living document

There is a place for AI to support some of this, such as synthesising consultation feedback. But it works best as a tool inside the process, not as a replacement for it.

# In short

If you are considering using AI to help you develop your organisational Strategy, ask a few questions: Who will we involve, and how will we make sure they feel heard? How will we make the tough choices about what we will and will not do? How will we build buy-in, and make sure what's written down is implemented? Does it sound like us?

If the answer to any of those is no, you don't have a Strategy yet. You have a good first draft, and there is nothing wrong with that, as long as the real work follows.
